Forward Deployed Engineering: From Business Problem to Working Product

By Abaid Ullah · Full Stack AI Software Engineer

Start with the handoff that breaks

A business problem often becomes visible where one person or system hands work to another. A customer requests a meeting, an operator copies the details into a calendar, and someone follows up manually. Before proposing automation, I would trace that journey and identify the owner, required information and failure points at each step. This makes the first deliverable a concrete workflow with acceptance criteria rather than a list of technologies.

My Snootme work included Nylas calendar integration and automated scheduling alongside AI functionality. That experience is relevant to teams connecting communication and operational tools. The useful engineering question is whether the workflow reaches a reliable business state: the meeting is created, the user receives clear feedback, and failures are visible to the team.

Deliver a small workflow that a client can evaluate

My documented delivery approach runs from requirements and specs through MVPs, staging, CI/CD and handoff. For a new integration project, I would first deliver one complete path using representative data. A client can then review what happens when information is missing, a provider is unavailable or a request changes. These examples expose ambiguity earlier than reviewing isolated screens.

Planet AI demonstrates my work on multi-agent workflow automation. For a similar engagement, I would explicitly identify which actions can run automatically and which need a person to review them. This is a product decision as well as an engineering decision: the operational owner needs to understand how to recover a failed task.

Choose automation around ownership and recovery

An integration creates an ongoing dependency on another system. My proposed design review would cover who owns credentials, how permission changes are handled, what happens to partially completed work and where the team can inspect failures. A simple integration with visible state can be more useful to an operations team than a complicated workflow it cannot troubleshoot.

SPS Fulfillment adds a logistics perspective to my portfolio: onboarding and shipment visibility serve different users and timelines. I would use the client’s actual onboarding steps to prioritize the first release, then review gaps with the people who operate the process each day.

Measure operational improvement with the client

For this type of project, I would establish a baseline for completion time, manual interventions, failed tasks and support requests. After release, I would compare the same workflow and user group over an agreed period. Faster execution would only count as an improvement if accuracy and recoverability remained acceptable.

Those are proposed evaluation measures, not published results for the projects above. My contribution is the documented integration and product engineering work. For a new client, I would turn the desired business outcome into a measurable acceptance target and include the operational handoff in the definition of done.
